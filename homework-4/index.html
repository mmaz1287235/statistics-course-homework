STATISTICS · HOMEWORK 04

Welford's Recurrence
and Sampling Variability

Computing a mean and a sample variance one observation at a time, then examining what happens when the number of penetration attempts increases.

Each hacker makes n attempts and receives one score per system. Attempts and hackers are independent in this model. The scores are simulated, not measurements from real networks.
01

Welford's recurrence

From Knuth's mean update to an online sum of squared deviations.

For observations x₁, …, xₙ, let Mₙ denote the mean and let SSₙ = Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ(xᵢ − Mₙ)². Start with n = 0, M₀ = 0 and SS₀ = 0. The mean update, with δₙ = xₙ − Mₙ₋₁, is:

Mₙ = Mₙ₋₁ + δₙ / n

For each previous observation, xᵢ − Mₙ = (xᵢ − Mₙ₋₁) − δₙ/n. Squaring and summing over i = 1, …, n − 1 gives SSₙ₋₁ + (n − 1)δₙ²/n². The cross term vanishes because Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ⁻¹(xᵢ − Mₙ₋₁) = 0. The new observation contributes (xₙ − Mₙ)² = ((n − 1)δₙ/n)². Therefore:

SSₙ = SSₙ₋₁ + (n − 1)δₙ²/n
= SSₙ₋₁ + δₙ(xₙ − Mₙ)

The unbiased sample variance uses the denominator n − 1:

sₙ² = SSₙ / (n − 1), n ≥ 2

Substituting SSₙ₋₁ = (n − 2)sₙ₋₁² also gives a direct variance recurrence for n ≥ 3:

sₙ² = [(n − 2)sₙ₋₁² + δₙ(xₙ − Mₙ)] / (n − 1)

At n = 2, use SS₂/1; s₁² is undefined. The running SS formulation works from the first observation and is the implementation used here.

The three-line update

const delta = x - mean;
mean += delta / count;
SS += delta * (x - mean);

Increment count before these three lines. The update uses constant memory, without keeping earlier observations.

03

Penetration score model

A fixed, deliberately simple probability distribution.

Discrete uniform scores

Each integer score from 0 through 10 has probability 1/11. The distribution is the same for every system and hacker.

P(X = k) = 1/11, k ∈ {0, 1, …, 10}

μ = E[X] = (0 + … + 10)/11 = 5

E[X²] = (0² + … + 10²)/11 = 35

σ² = E[X²] − μ² = 35 − 25 = 10

Why this model?

Integer scores are easy to interpret: 0 represents no achieved penetration score and 10 is the maximum score on our scale. Uniform probabilities are a modelling choice, not a claim about actual attacks.

For independent scores, the sample mean estimates μ and the corrected sample variance estimates σ². Both are unbiased when n ≥ 2.

05

What the results tell us

Expected patterns, statistical meaning, and limits.

Comparison with the population parameters

For every n, E[Mₙ] = μ = 5 and E[sₙ²] = σ² = 10. Averaging these estimates over many independent hackers should bring the Monte Carlo averages close to 5 and 10. They need not match exactly in any particular run. More hackers reduce the Monte Carlo noise of those averages.

What changes when n grows?

Var(Mₙ) = σ²/n = 10/n. The distribution of individual hacker means therefore becomes narrower as n increases. The corrected sample variance also becomes more stable, though its variability depends on the fourth moment of the score distribution:

Var(sₙ²) = [μ₄ − ((n − 3)/(n − 1))σ⁴] / n
μ₄ = E[(X − 5)⁴] = 178; σ⁴ = 100

These are theoretical variances of estimators, not the score variance σ². A single hacker's estimate can still move away from the target when one more score is observed; the reduction concerns the overall sampling variability.

Why this matters for inference

We can use a sample to estimate a population mean or variance without storing every observation. Unbiasedness describes the estimators' expectations; consistency describes how their uncertainty shrinks as sample size increases. The theoretical spread of estimates helps us quantify estimation error and eventually build confidence intervals, provided the model assumptions are justified.

Limits of this experiment

Scores are simulated as independent draws from a known uniform distribution. Actual penetration attempts may differ in difficulty, share vulnerabilities, and depend on previous attempts. The conclusions here illustrate sampling theory under the stated model, not the behaviour of a real network.
