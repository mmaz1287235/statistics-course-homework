An experiment with elliptic-curve scalar multiplication, using a deliberately small curve to make the arithmetic visible.

What this models. Ethereum uses the secp256k1 elliptic curve to relate private and public keys. Here we work with a much smaller curve, so every point can be inspected. This example is for learning only; it cannot protect real keys.

What the results mean

Some statistical ideas apply to this mapping, but they should not be confused with the security requirements of a hash function.

Determinism and the one-way idea

For a chosen private key k, the output Q = kG is always the same. Computing Q is straightforward when the curve and G are known. With a real, sufficiently large curve and a suitable subgroup, recovering k from Q is believed to be computationally infeasible. This is the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem. The small curve used here offers no such protection: all possible outputs can be listed immediately.

Uniformity and repetition

When k is sampled uniformly over the nonzero residues modulo 9, each of the eight finite multiples of G appears once, together with one point at infinity. That is uniformity over the cyclic subgroup, not uniformity of individual x and y coordinates. In the plot and frequency table, several coordinate values recur. Key choices outside a single cycle simply repeat the same points.

Input/output correlation

The mapping from k to kG is algebraic and periodic. A numerical correlation between k and a coordinate would depend on the chosen encoding and the small sample; it would not measure the difficulty of the discrete logarithm problem. Here the full sequence is more informative than an isolated correlation coefficient.

Bit patterns and avalanche-like changes

We count 1s in five-bit binary encodings of the coordinates and compare consecutive finite outputs with Hamming distance. These are descriptive measurements, but they depend on the coordinate representation. The group operation does not promise that changing one bit of k will flip half the bits of Q. The usual avalanche criterion is associated with cryptographic hash functions and block-cipher components, not a formal requirement of elliptic-curve scalar multiplication.

Why this is not a hash function

Elliptic-curve scalar multiplication maps a scalar into a curve point. Its intended security relies on the difficulty of reversing that group operation. A cryptographic hash function instead maps messages of arbitrary length to fixed-length digests and is evaluated using properties such as preimage resistance, second-preimage resistance and collision resistance. We should not infer those properties from the coordinate histograms of this toy curve.

Connection to Ethereum

Ethereum externally owned accounts use public keys derived from secp256k1 private keys. The Ethereum address is generated by hashing the uncompressed public key coordinates with Keccak-256 and taking the last 20 bytes. That address derivation is another operation, not part of the toy point multiplication implemented on this page.

Limit of the experiment. Eight finite points cannot establish anything about real-world cryptographic security. The purpose of the statistics is to identify patterns, explain what they measure, and see where a small mathematical example differs from cryptographic practice.
