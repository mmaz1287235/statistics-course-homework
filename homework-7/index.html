From Convergence to Bayesian Inference

How random observations become reliable statistical information, and why independence matters when learning from data.

Learning objectives: distinguish convergence modes, derive the square-root scaling, and assess how prior information and dependence affect inference.

All numerical scenarios are synthetic teaching examples. The simulations illustrate mathematical results; they do not prove them or establish empirical claims about network systems.
01

Modes of convergence

Four distinct meanings of approaching a limit.

Let Xₙ and X be real random variables on a common probability space, except that convergence in distribution only requires their laws. For mean-square convergence assume Xₙ and X belong to L², the space of square-integrable random variables. The following definitions distinguish the behavior of individual outcomes, probabilities, moments and distributions.

ModeDefinitionInterpretation and role
In probabilityP(|Xₙ − X| > ε) → 0 for every ε > 0Large deviations become unlikely; weak consistency and WLLN.
Almost surely (a.s.)P({ω: Xₙ(ω) → X(ω)}) = 1Entire sample paths converge outside a null set; SLLN.
Mean-square (L²)E[(Xₙ − X)²] → 0Squared error vanishes in expectation; estimator risk.
In distributionFₙ(x) → F(x) at every continuity point of FOnly the laws converge; CLT and asymptotic inference.

More precisely, the downward implication starts at convergence in probability. Almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability by dominated convergence applied to the deviation indicators. Markov's inequality gives P(|Xₙ−X| > ε) ≤ E[(Xₙ−X)²]/ε², proving the L² implication. Convergence in probability implies convergence in distribution: bound Fₙ(x) between F(x−ε)−P(|Xₙ−X|>ε) and F(x+ε)+P(|Xₙ−X|>ε), then let ε decrease to zero at continuity points. Convergence in distribution to a constant also implies convergence in probability. Conversely, if Xₙ → X almost surely and |Xₙ| ≤ Y for all n with E[Y²] finite, then |X| ≤ Y almost surely and dominated convergence gives E[|Xₙ−X|²] → 0.

Why the reverse implications fail

Probability but not almost surely: independent Bernoulli variables with success probability 1/n converge in probability to zero, but successes occur infinitely often almost surely by the second Borel–Cantelli lemma.

Almost surely but not L²: on (0,1) with uniform U, let Xₙ = √n · 1{U < 1/n}. For every U > 0 the sequence eventually vanishes, yet E[Xₙ²] = 1.

L² but not almost surely: the independent Bernoulli example above has E[Xₙ²] = 1/n, yet fails almost sure convergence.

Distribution but not probability: if X is a symmetric random sign and Xₙ = −X for every n, their distributions coincide, but |Xₙ−X| = 2 always.

Probability but not L²: the shrinking-interval example above also converges in probability, while its second moment stays equal to one.

02

The law of large numbers

Stability of the sample mean.

For i.i.d. observations with E[|X₁|] finite and mean μ, the weak law gives X̄ₙ → μ in probability and the strong law gives X̄ₙ → μ almost surely. Under the additional finite variance assumption σ², E[X̄ₙ]=μ and E[(X̄ₙ−μ)²]=σ²/n, proving mean-square convergence. Chebyshev then proves the weak law:

P(|X̄ₙ − μ| > ε) ≤ σ²/(n ε²) → 0.

For a tolerance ε, the Chebyshev bound concerns a single sample size, not simultaneous control of all later means. The strong law is a statement about whole trajectories, not just the probability at a fixed n; this Chebyshev argument alone does not prove it.

03

The central limit theorem

Fluctuations around the limiting mean.

For i.i.d. variables with finite mean and finite positive variance, the classical CLT states:

Zₙ = √n (X̄ₙ − μ)/σ → N(0,1) in distribution.

The LLN identifies the limiting mean, whereas the CLT describes the shape of the rescaled error. The unscaled mean converges to a constant; Zₙ retains unit variance. Under suitable regularity and sampling assumptions, replacing σ by a consistent estimator is justified by Slutsky's theorem, enabling approximate confidence intervals and tests.

CLT laboratory

Compare the empirical density of 2,000 standardized sample means with the standard normal density. Skewness in the exponential population makes the finite-sample approximation visible. For independent observations with finite third moment, the standardized sum has skewness γ₁(X)/√n: 2/√n for the exponential population and zero for the symmetric uniform population. Zero skewness alone does not establish normality.

04

Why √n? Independence and dependence

Variance addition and the limits of precision.

Var(Σ Xᵢ) = Σ Var(Xᵢ) + 2 Σᵢ<ⱼ Cov(Xᵢ,Xⱼ)

Independence implies zero covariance when second moments exist. Pairwise uncorrelatedness suffices for variance addition, but does not establish independence or the classical CLT. For independent observations of common variance σ², Var(Σ Xᵢ)=nσ² and Var(X̄ₙ)=σ²/n. The standard error is σ/√n; quadrupling n halves it. Dividing the centered sum by σ√n produces variance one. This explains the normalization, although the normal limiting shape requires the CLT's assumptions.

Var(X̄ₙ) = Var(Σ Xᵢ)/n² = nσ²/n² = σ²/n
SD(X̄ₙ) = σ/√n

A shared network condition

Let Xᵢ = μ + σ(√ρ Z + √(1−ρ) εᵢ), with 0 ≤ ρ ≤ 1 and σ > 0, where Z and the εᵢ are mutually independent standard normal variables. Each observation has variance σ² and each distinct pair has covariance ρσ². This models a persistent shared congestion offset in a measurement session.

Var(X̄ₙ) = σ² [ρ + (1−ρ)/n]

Averaging the model gives X̄ₙ = μ + σ√ρ Z + σ√(1−ρ) ε̄ₙ. The strong law implies ε̄ₙ → 0 almost surely. For ρ > 0, the variance approaches ρσ² and the sample mean converges almost surely to μ+σ√ρ Z, not to μ. This is a particular dependence model; other weakly dependent sequences can still satisfy LLNs and CLTs, with a different long-run variance. Naive independent-observation confidence intervals can be too narrow.

Effective sample size

Equating the correlated variance to σ²/neff gives the equivalent independent sample size in terms of variance, not a literal count of independent observations.

neff = n/[1+(n−1)ρ]

Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem

Updating uncertainty using observations.

For P(B)>0, P(A|B)=P(A∩B)/P(B). If P(A) is also positive, the product rule gives P(A∩B) = P(A|B)P(B) = P(B|A)P(A). Dividing by P(B) yields Bayes’ theorem:

P(A|B) = P(B|A) P(A) / P(B)

For mutually exclusive and exhaustive hypotheses Hⱼ, the denominator is Σⱼ P(B|Hⱼ)P(Hⱼ). For two complementary hypotheses, with positive prior probabilities, this gives the following expanded form. In statistical inference, the hypotheses may instead be values of an unknown parameter θ.

P(A|B) = P(B|A)P(A) / [P(B|A)P(A)+P(B|Aᶜ)P(Aᶜ)]

The denominator is the marginal likelihood, assumed finite and positive. For discrete parameters replace the integral with a sum. Updating can be sequential: the current posterior becomes the next prior, and the next likelihood must condition on previous evidence if observations are dependent. In frequentist inference a fixed parameter is not assigned a probability distribution; conditional probability still underlies sampling models and likelihoods.

p(θ|D₁,D₂) ∝ p(D₂|θ,D₁) p(θ|D₁)

Under conditional independence of D₁ and D₂ given θ can the first factor be replaced by p(D₂|θ). A frequentist 95% confidence procedure has 95% coverage under repeated sampling (when its assumptions hold); a Bayesian 95% credible interval has posterior probability 0.95 under the specified model.

Bayesian network latency estimation

A continuous Normal–Normal model.

Suppose a service's mean latency μ has prior N(m₀,τ₀²). Conditional on μ, measurements are independent N(μ,σ²), with known σ=20 ms. The observed sample mean starts at 70 ms and can be varied in this sensitivity exercise. The Gaussian model is an approximation for a stable operating regime, not a universal latency distribution; negative values and heavy tails can make it unsuitable.

L(μ) ∝ exp[−n(μ−X̄ₙ)²/(2σ²)]
τₙ² = (1/τ₀² + n/σ²)⁻¹
mₙ = τₙ²(m₀/τ₀² + n X̄ₙ/σ²)

Multiplying the prior and likelihood adds their quadratic exponents. Define A = 1/τ₀² + n/σ² and B = m₀/τ₀² + nX̄ₙ/σ². The exponent, up to a constant independent of μ, is −(Aμ²−2Bμ)/2 = −A(μ−B/A)²/2 plus a constant. Completing the square gives μ|D ∼ N(mₙ,τₙ²). The posterior mean is a precision-weighted average of prior and sample means. The posterior standard deviation approaches σ/√n for a fixed proper prior as n increases, linking Bayesian concentration to sampling uncertainty. Indeed, τₙ/(σ/√n) = [1+σ²/(nτ₀²)]⁻¹/² → 1. This is an exact model-specific result, not a universal guarantee. The weights of prior and data are τₙ²/τ₀² and nτₙ²/σ²; they sum to one.

Bayesian updating laboratory

The likelihood is rescaled to unit integral solely for comparing shapes. A 95% credible interval has posterior probability 0.95 under this model; it is not a frequentist confidence interval by definition.

07

Dependent evidence and Bayesian updating

Two monitoring systems, one shared source.

Consider a hypothetical service degradation H with prior probability p. Each monitor flags degradation with probability a if H is true and b otherwise (defaults 0.8 and 0.2). Conditional on either state, with probability q the two monitors share one Bernoulli signal; otherwise they generate independent signals. Their individual alert rates remain unchanged.

P(A₁∩A₂|H) = qa + (1−q)a²
P(A₁∩A₂|¬H) = qb + (1−q)b²
P(H|A₁∩A₂) = p J₁ / [p J₁ + (1−p) J₀]

Here J₁ and J₀ are the respective joint alert probabilities. At q=0 the alerts are conditionally independent; at q=1 they are duplicates. When a > b, treating positively shared evidence as independent exaggerates the likelihood ratio in this model; when a < b, the direction reverses. Conditional independence is different from unconditional independence: the shared unknown state can induce marginal association even when q=0.

LR = J₁/J₀
Posterior odds = [p/(1−p)] × LR

A shared signal contributes less independent information than two separate signals. In the laboratory, each monitor has the same marginal alert rates conditional on the state.

08

Conclusions and limitations

More observations are useful only under defensible assumptions.

Convergence in probability supports consistency, almost sure convergence gives pathwise stability, L² convergence controls squared risk, and distributional convergence supports approximate sampling inference. The √n scale reflects variance addition under suitable conditions. The shared-factor example demonstrates why correlated observations can invalidate the usual standard error.

Bayesian inference combines prior knowledge and observed evidence in a coherent probability model and can support sequential learning, uncertainty quantification and decisions. Its conclusions are sensitive to prior choices, likelihood misspecification, dependence and selection effects. A strong but inaccurate prior can dominate sparse data; sensitivity analysis and model checks are essential. Complex posterior distributions may also require computational approximations. Neither Bayesian nor frequentist inference automatically compensates for biased data.

Practical implications

Bayesian models enable explicit incorporation of historical information and sequential updates, but require defensible priors and likelihoods. In network measurements, shared congestion can reduce effective information; duplicate alerts can similarly inflate posterior odds. Compare alternative priors, inspect model fit and validate dependence assumptions before acting on estimates.
